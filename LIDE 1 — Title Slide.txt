SLIDE 3 — The WiFi Congestion Problem
The problem begins with a rule built into every WiFi device. The IEEE 802.11 standard — the global specification that governs how WiFi works — instructs every laptop, phone, and tablet to connect to whichever access point broadcasts the strongest signal. This rule is called RSSI-first association. It was designed to maximise individual link quality, but it has a serious consequence at the network level. Because every device in a room follows the same rule and the same physical access point tends to have the strongest signal, they all connect to that one AP simultaneously. The result is that one access point can accumulate 80 or more connected devices while physically nearby access points with significant available capacity sit almost completely unused.
This imbalance has a direct and measurable effect on speed. The Shannon-Hartley Theorem — which I will derive in the next slide — proves that a wireless channel has a fixed maximum data rate that must be divided equally among all connected users. When 80 students share a channel with a practical capacity of approximately 84 megabits per second, each student receives on average only 1.05 megabits per second. To put that in context, downloading a 50 megabyte software package — a standard task in a programming laboratory — requires a minimum of 4 megabits per second to complete within 2 minutes. At 1.05 megabits per second, that same download takes over 6 minutes, and under real congestion conditions it often fails entirely. The consequences are failed downloads, broken online examination submissions, and lost instructional time that lecturers cannot recover.
The root cause is straightforward but important: signal strength does not equal network performance. A device can have an excellent signal to an overloaded access point and a slightly weaker signal to a nearly empty one. The standard gives no mechanism for the device to know the difference.

SLIDE 4 — First-Principles Engineering Analysis
To prove this is a complex engineering problem and not simply a matter of adding more bandwidth, I analysed it using three mathematical models from first principles.
The first is the Shannon-Hartley Theorem. This theorem states that the maximum data rate C on any communication channel equals the bandwidth B multiplied by the logarithm base 2 of one plus the signal-to-noise ratio. For a standard IEEE 802.11n channel with 20 megahertz of bandwidth and a signal-to-noise ratio of 28 decibels — which corresponds to a typical measured RSSI of negative 65 dBm in a university classroom — the theoretical maximum capacity is 186 megabits per second. After accounting for protocol overhead, guard intervals, and the CSMA/CA contention mechanism, practical capacity drops to approximately 84 megabits per second per access point. This is a hard physical limit. No configuration change can exceed it.
The second model is the M/M/1 queueing model from queueing theory. This model describes how packets wait to be served at an access point. The mean packet waiting time W equals one divided by the service rate minus the arrival rate. What this formula reveals is that latency does not increase linearly with load — it increases exponentially as the access point approaches saturation. At 15 users, mean waiting time is 4 milliseconds. At 19 users it is 20 milliseconds. At 20 users, the queue collapses entirely and waiting time approaches infinity. This non-linear behaviour is why latency receives the second-highest weight in the scoring formula.
The third analysis uses the Free-Space Path Loss equation from electromagnetic theory. This formula calculates the signal loss between a transmitter and receiver based on distance and frequency. At a typical classroom distance of 10 metres on a 2.4 gigahertz channel with standard wall attenuation, the predicted RSSI is approximately negative 49 dBm. This calculation validates the normalisation range of negative 30 to negative 90 dBm that the scoring algorithm uses, confirming that the range is physically justified rather than arbitrarily chosen.
Finally, this problem satisfies the ECSA definition of a complex engineering problem: it involves multiple interacting subsystems from different engineering disciplines, it presents conflicting technical constraints, and it cannot be resolved through any existing standard configuration procedure.

With the problem and its engineering analysis established, I can now present the system I designed to address it.

SLIDE 6 — Five Engineering Alternatives Evaluated
Before selecting a solution, I identified and evaluated five distinct engineering alternatives. Each one addresses the same root cause — RSSI-only AP association — but from a completely different engineering perspective.
Alternative 1 is deploying additional Wi-Fi 6 hardware. Wi-Fi 6 introduces Multi-User MIMO technology that allows one access point to serve multiple users on separate spatial streams simultaneously, which would increase per-AP capacity. However, this requires physically installing new hardware throughout every laboratory, which costs between 80 and 200 thousand rand per laboratory and requires full infrastructure access.
Alternative 2 is an Enterprise Wireless LAN Controller from vendors such as Cisco or Aruba. A centralised controller can monitor the load on every AP in real time and force devices to roam to less-loaded APs. Published research by Bejerano and Kokku showed this approach can achieve up to 100 percent throughput improvement. However, enterprise controllers cost over 150 thousand rand, require complex integration with the existing network switch fabric, and demand exclusive administrative access to the campus network.
Alternative 3 is modifying the AP firmware using open-source firmware such as OpenWRT to implement load-balancing algorithms at the AP level. This would work technically, but it requires flashing third-party firmware onto university-owned access points, which voids equipment warranties and requires administrative access to every AP.
Alternative 4 is the IEEE 802.11k and 802.11v protocol amendments, which enable AP-assisted roaming. Under these protocols, access points can share radio resource information and request that devices move to less-loaded APs. This is a standards-based approach, but it requires both the campus APs and every student device to support the protocol extensions, plus Wireless LAN Controller configuration — none of which are available on most university deployments without administrative access.
Alternative 5, which was selected, is an on-device intelligent monitoring and recommendation system. It requires zero capital expenditure, no changes to any infrastructure, and no administrative access of any kind. It is the only alternative that satisfies all eight user requirements within the constraints of this project.

SLIDE 7 — Selected Solution: On-Device Monitoring System
The selected solution is a passive software tool that runs entirely on the user's own laptop. Passive means it only reads information that the surrounding WiFi environment broadcasts publicly — it never modifies any network settings, never forces any connections, and never transmits any data to external servers.
The system works in three stages. First, it observes the WiFi environment by using operating system commands — specifically netsh on Windows and iwlist on Linux — to scan all nearby access points and collect their SSID names, BSSID hardware addresses, signal strengths, and channel information. Second, it analyses connection quality by sending 10 controlled ICMP ping packets to the default gateway and measuring the mean latency, packet loss percentage, and round-trip time variance of the current connection. Third, it recommends action by applying the weighted scoring formula to produce a quality score from 0 to 100 for each detected access point and displaying a recommendation message when a significantly better AP is available.
Critically, the system only fires a recommendation when the better AP's score exceeds the current AP's score by at least 20 points. This threshold is not arbitrary — it is calculated to be larger than the combined measurement uncertainty of plus or minus 12 points, which means the system never recommends switching based on noise alone. The entire recommendation is advisory: the user decides whether to act on it.

SLIDE 8 — System Architecture: Five Subsystems
The system is structured as five clearly defined software subsystems, each implemented as a separate Python module, communicating through typed Python dataclass interfaces.
The first subsystem is the Sensing module, implemented in scanner.py. It invokes the netsh OS command via Python subprocess, parses the raw text output using regular expressions, and converts the Windows signal quality percentage to RSSI in decibels per milliwatt using the formula RSSI equals signal percentage divided by two minus 100 — a derivation specified in the Windows NDIS driver documentation.
The second is the Network Testing module in tester.py. It sends 10 ICMP packets to the default gateway, which isolates local access point congestion from variability in the wider internet connection. It computes mean latency, packet loss percentage, RTT variance as a stability proxy, and jitter per RFC 3393.
The third is the Scoring module in scorer.py. It normalises all four metrics to a common 0 to 100 scale using clamp formulas with bounds derived from first principles, applies the weighted scoring formula, classifies each AP, and evaluates whether the recommendation threshold is exceeded.
The fourth is the Data Storage module in storage.py. It appends one 18-column CSV row per access point per monitoring cycle to a timestamped session file stored locally on the device, consuming approximately 11.5 megabytes for a full 8-hour session.
The fifth is the User Interface module in dashboard.py. It renders a Streamlit web application at localhost port 8501, displaying a colour-coded AP comparison table, KPI summary cards, a recommendation message box, a signal strength bar chart, and a 30-second latency trend graph. The entire dashboard auto-refreshes every 10 seconds via the Streamlit rerun mechanism.
The monitoring loop repeats every 10 seconds throughout the session. This interval was chosen because the operating system's WiFi scan itself takes 2 to 4 seconds, meaning shorter intervals would not yield new data.

SLIDE 9 — Intelligent Scoring Algorithm
The scoring formula is the engineering heart of the system. It takes four raw measurements — each on a completely different physical scale — and converts them into a single comparable quality score.
The formula is: Score equals 0.35 times the normalised signal score, plus 0.30 times the normalised latency score, plus 0.20 times the normalised packet loss score, plus 0.15 times the normalised stability score.
Signal strength receives 35 percent because it is the most universally available metric and directly reflects the electromagnetic link quality between the device and the access point. Its normalisation maps RSSI from negative 30 dBm — excellent signal — down to negative 90 dBm — no signal — using bounds validated by the Free-Space Path Loss derivation.
Latency receives 30 percent because the M/M/1 model shows that latency is the metric most sensitive to congestion near saturation. A small increase in user load near capacity causes a catastrophic and non-linear increase in waiting time, making latency the most reliable indicator of severe congestion.
Packet loss receives 20 percent because the TCP protocol responds to every lost packet by retransmitting it, effectively halving the useful throughput for each retransmission event. High packet loss has a compounding negative effect on perceived speed.
Connection stability, measured as the variance of round-trip times, receives 15 percent because a highly variable connection — even one with acceptable average latency — causes application-level failures such as video freezing, session timeouts, and upload errors.
The final score maps to three classifications based on ITU-T Y.1540 quality standards: a score of 75 or above is classified as GOOD, meaning per-user throughput exceeds the 4 megabits per second threshold. A score between 50 and 74 is MODERATE — acceptable but degrading. A score below 50 is CONGESTED, meaning throughput has collapsed below 2.8 megabits per second and action is needed.

SLIDE 10 — Real-Time Dashboard Interface
The dashboard is designed so that any student, without any technical knowledge, can understand their connection status and know what to do within seconds of opening the application.
At the top of the screen are five KPI summary cards showing the current access point name, the composite quality score out of 100, the live status badge showing GOOD, MODERATE, or CONGESTED, the current latency in milliseconds, and the total number of scans completed in this session.
Below the KPI cards is the AP comparison table. Every access point detected in the current scan is listed with its hardware address, signal strength in dBm, latency, packet loss percentage, composite score, and a colour-coded status badge. The currently connected AP is highlighted. This gives the user a direct comparison of all available options.
When the system detects a better AP with a score at least 20 points higher than the current connection, a recommendation alert box appears in amber, stating which AP to connect to and giving directional guidance on where to move within the room.
Below the table are two charts: a bar chart showing signal strength per detected AP, and a line graph showing the latency trend for the last 30 seconds. The trend graph allows the user to see at a glance whether their connection is improving or worsening over time, which is particularly useful when they are deciding whether to move to a different location.
All data is stored locally on the device. Nothing is transmitted to any external server, satisfying both User Requirement 8 and the Protection of Personal Information Act requirements that apply to the University of Johannesburg.

SLIDE 11 — Section Divider: Testing and Conclusions
With the system fully specified, I will now present the validation testing plan and the conclusions from this project.

SLIDE 12 — Validation Testing Plan
The system will be validated through five testing phases that progressively simulate the high-density laboratory environment described in the problem statement.
Phase 1 tests low user density with 1 to 5 devices connected. In this phase, the system should report a GOOD status for all detected access points in at least 8 out of 10 consecutive monitoring cycles.
Phase 2 tests high user density with 15 or more devices connected to the same AP simultaneously. The system should correctly classify the AP as CONGESTED — a score below 50 — and should issue a recommendation if a better AP is available with a score gap of at least 20 points.
Phase 3 tests the system's responsiveness to physical movement. As the user moves from an overloaded AP towards a better one, the score should shift by at least 10 points in the correct direction across 3 or more consecutive cycles. This confirms the system is tracking real changes in the wireless environment rather than producing static readings.
Phase 4 tests multi-AP ranking accuracy. With 3 or more access points simultaneously visible, the system should correctly rank all of them by score and identify the best one as the recommended option.
Phase 5 is a 30-minute continuous operation test. The system must run without throwing any software exceptions, and the CSV log file must grow at approximately 7,200 rows per hour — which confirms that the storage subsystem is recording every measurement from every AP in every cycle correctly.
In addition to the five integration phases, the detailed design specifies 9 individual unit tests covering boundary-value conditions for the normalisation formulas, the classification thresholds, and the recommendation logic.
